🔭 Australia's Most Sighted Birds

Australia supports extraordinary bird diversity over 900 species, roughly 45% of which are found nowhere else on Earth. The charts below highlight which species birdwatchers encounter most often and which are reported most consistently across survey sites. High sighting frequency does not always mean high conservation value; some common urban species dominate the count while rarer shorebirds appear more selectively at specialised coastal habitats.

🐦
Magpie Dominance
The Australian Magpie consistently tops sighting counts across all states, reflecting its adaptability to urban, suburban, and rural environments alike. Its high report rate signals that it is reliably encountered whenever a survey is conducted.
📊
Report Rate vs Raw Count
A high report rate (right chart) is a stronger indicator of ubiquity than raw sighting counts. Species like the Welcome Swallow and Willie Wagtail achieve near-equal report rates to the Magpie despite lower absolute counts, suggesting they occupy a similarly wide range of habitats but in smaller flock sizes.
🏙️
Urban Bias in the Data
Citizen science data inherently over-represents species near population centres. The top-ranked birds are almost exclusively those tolerant of human activity. This is not a failing it is a known characteristic of opportunistic survey data that analysts must account for when drawing conservation conclusions.

🌏 Shorebird Migration Patterns Over Time

Every year, millions of shorebirds undertake one of nature's most extraordinary journeys, flying up to 10,000 km non-stop from breeding grounds in Siberia, Alaska, and China to reach the warm mudflats and estuaries of Australia. They arrive in the austral spring (October–November), fatten on invertebrates throughout summer, then depart in March–April. Seasonal & Classification Breakdown
📉
A Concerning Downward Trend
Peak count data from 2010 to 2024 reveals a gradual decline in several key shorebird species. Far Eastern Curlew and Curlew Sandpiper, both critically listed, show the steepest trajectories, consistent with global assessments that East Asian–Australasian Flyway shorebirds face mounting habitat loss along their migration routes.
🍂
Sharp Seasonal Pulse
The seasonal chart reveals a pronounced spike in shorebird counts between October and March (Australia's summer). Records outside this window represent resident species or early/late migrants. This tight seasonality underscores how critically dependent these birds are on consistent intertidal feeding conditions upon arrival after their non-stop transoceanic flights.
🥧
81% Nationally Important
The donut chart shows that 81% of all shorebird records in this dataset meet Australia's national importance threshold, meaning they represent ≥1% of the national population at that site. The remaining 19% meet the even higher international threshold (≥1% of the global flyway population), concentrated almost exclusively in Western Australia.

🗺️ Where Do Shorebirds Gather?

Shorebird abundance across Australia is strikingly uneven. Western Australia alone accounts for over 2.18 million peak bird counts more than three times the combined total of all other states. This dominance is driven by globally significant sites like Eighty Mile Beach and Roebuck Bay, which provide vast, undisturbed intertidal mudflats. The choropleth map, scatter plot, and bubble chart below expose the full spatial picture, from continental scale down to individual sites.

State & Species Overview
🏆
WA's Outsized Importance
Western Australia's northwest coastline provides some of the largest undisturbed intertidal habitats on the planet. Eighty Mile Beach alone hosts peak counts exceeding 1.4 million birds, more than the entirety of New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, and South Australia combined. Any significant development or sea-level change in this region would have global consequences for flyway populations.
⚖️
National vs International Thresholds
The scatter plot distinguishes sites that clear Australia's national importance threshold (≥1% of national population) from those clearing the higher international bar (≥1% of global flyway population). Most internationally important sites cluster in WA and NT, while southern states contain nationally important sites that are nonetheless crucial for local breeding and stopover refuelling.
🫧
Species Richness vs Abundance
The bubble chart reveals that high abundance and high species richness do not always coincide. Some states host diverse assemblages of species at moderate densities, while WA concentrates enormous numbers of fewer dominant species. This distinction matters for conservation planning, a state can lose critical abundance without losing species count, which standard biodiversity metrics would miss.

🔬 A Local Lens: Bird Sightings in Greater Geelong

Zooming into Greater Geelong brings the national story into sharp local focus. With over 65,000 sighting records spanning 8 common species, Geelong's parks, coastline, and suburban green spaces support a surprisingly rich avian community. The Australian Magpie leads local counts (1,907 records), followed closely by the Red Wattlebird and Welcome Swallow. Use the species dropdown on the map to explore habitat preferences, and the brush on the area chart to examine year-by-year sighting trends.

Sightings Over Time
🌿
Clustering Reveals Habitat Preference
The dot map shows clearly that species like the Superb Fairy-wren and Red Wattlebird cluster in vegetated corridors and residential gardens, while species like the Willie Wagtail spread more evenly across open areas near water. These spatial signatures help urban planners identify which green infrastructure investments most benefit local bird diversity.
📈
Citizen Science Effort Effect
The area chart likely shows a rise in sighting records after 2015. This was not because more birds arrived, but because more observers began contributing data. Distinguishing genuine population change from increased observer effort is a key analytical challenge when interpreting long-term citizen science trends.
🦢
Geelong as a Biodiversity Corridor
Greater Geelong's position on Port Phillip Bay and the Bellarine Peninsula places it within a broader coastal corridor for both resident and migratory species. The even spread of eight common species across the region, each with roughly 1,400–1,900 records, suggests a relatively balanced urban ecosystem, neither dominated by a single species nor depleted of diversity.
Key Takeaway
Australia's shorebirds face a dual challenge: habitat degradation at their northern wintering grounds and climate-driven change to their southern non-breeding sites. The data consistently points to Western Australia as the nation's most critical stronghold.
